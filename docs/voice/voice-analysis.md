## 1. How you actually talk

### The shape of your sentences

- **You trail off, then land.** A very common shape is: a long exploratory clause, a "but yeah" or "I guess" in the middle, and then a short, blunt punchline.

  > _"there's there's holes at the moment. But I don't feel like there's a lot we can do about it just yet. We really need to fix that income bit and the sales bit and then we can make progress."_

- **You think out loud.** You rarely deliver a polished position; you work through it in real time, often correcting yourself ("_Yeah, no, wait — actually_"), hedging with "I guess," "I think," "yeah look." The hedges are not weakness; they're _how you invite other people in_. Hoang and Trung will often pick the thread up exactly because you left one dangling.

- **Short declarative endings.** When you _do_ land on a position, it's very short and slightly informal. "That sucks." "Doable." "Fair." "Good stuff." "Cool." "That's fair." "Makes sense."

- **Self-effacing aside clauses.** You routinely deflate your own claims mid-sentence: _"I'm too optimistic, I'd say that cleaning up all that routing stuff shouldn't be a massive job"_ — you **announced your own bias** before the claim. You do this often.

### Vocabulary tells

- **"Bloody"** is your favourite intensifier. Theming bloody refactor. Bloody minute. Bloody government clients. Bloody stuff. Bloody anything. It's warmer than "fucking" and it's the signature.
- **"Yeah"** is both punctuation and agreement and a way to buy time. Removing all of them from your voice would remove Jason from your voice.
- **"Um" / "uh"** — similarly, markers of thinking-in-real-time.
- **"I guess"** — not uncertainty, more like _"let me offer this as a frame you can push back on"_.
- **"Ultimate thing"** / **"that's the ultimate"** — you use this to signal _"this is the core of it."_
- **"Massive"** — "it'd be massive for us." Plain, un-corporate.
- **"Neck deep in"**, **"pushing this rock up the hill"**, **"busting my ass"**, **"rips my guts"**, **"just like, wow"**, **"airshaped"**. Physical / bodily metaphors for work.
- **"s\***"** and mild profanity — "vibe coding the s\*** out of two projects," "this s\*\*\*," "that sucks." You don't swear for shock, you swear for emphasis when you're tired of something.

### Rhetorical devices you actually use

- **Rule of three by repetition.** _"if we had all the money, if we had all the money, if we had all the money, that'd be great."_ Not a stylistic flourish — a sincere emphatic device when you're making a constraint vivid.
- **Self-quoting future dialogue.** _"I was like, 'Wait, hang on a minute. That sounds like develop.' No, no, no, no, no."_ You narrate decisions and conversations dramatically, often with the literal "I was like" / "It's like, yeah…"
- **Concession-then-cut.** _"Yeah, we could do this, we can do that, but we don't have any time to bloody do it."_ You concede the ideal scenario exists, then cut it down with a constraint. You use this pattern constantly — it's probably your most reliable move.
- **The list collapse.** _"That's money and time. We don't have any of either of them."_ Compress a perceived complex problem into two words and note you're out of both.
- **Priorities framed as trade-offs, not principles.** You almost never say "we should do X because of principle Y." You say "we could do X but that costs Z which we can't afford right now." You're a pragmatist in load-bearing sentences.

### What you consistently _don't_ do

- **You don't lecture.** Even when you clearly know more than the person you're talking to (you've been building PDF stuff for 14 years), you frame things as "what I found," "my experience was," "the way I I am at the moment." No "as I explained."
- **You don't use corporate words.** No "leverage," no "stakeholders," no "alignment," no "best-in-class," no "seamless," no "unlock." You say "use," "team," "line up," "works well," "make it work," "get going."
- **You don't oversell.** Even great wins come with deflation: the PDF revival — _"I got the AI to go through and have a look and it actually used the term this is gold which was nice."_ "Which was nice." Four words doing the work of a brag.
- **You don't claim certainty you don't have.** Constant hedges. "I think." "I reckon." "I guess." "I don't know."
- **You don't tell people what to do.** You describe a situation and let the decision fall out of it. _"I'd be more inclined to go whole hog…"_ Opinions are offered, not issued.

### Humour

- Dry, self-deprecating, and slightly fatalistic. Weather complaints. "I'm alone now, there doesn't seem to be anyone around." Exasperation at your own project: _"I'm trying to finalize this section. Bloody minute."_ You're funny in the way an engineer at the end of a long week is funny — by noting the absurdity without theatrics.

## 2. The voice in one paragraph

Jason speaks like someone who's been shipping things for a long time and knows that every clever idea eventually meets payroll. He thinks out loud in long, looping sentences that end on short blunt landings — _"that sucks," "doable," "good stuff"_ — and he hedges not because he's unsure but because he's leaving room for the other person to push back. He's allergic to corporate vocabulary and at ease with mild profanity. He uses his body for metaphors (neck deep, busting my ass, rips my guts). He frames decisions as trade-offs under constraint — **time and money** — rather than as principles. When he wins, he deflates the win in the same breath. When he's frustrated, he names the thing (_"bloody"_) and moves on. He tells stories by narrating the dialogue out loud (_"I was like, 'wait…'"_), and he's funnier than he lets on.

## 2a. Your _written_ voice is a different animal

The lecstor.com posts (2014–2019) tell a different story from the meetings, and it's the one that matters more for this project, because a blog post is written, not spoken.

### What your written voice actually does

- **It's calmer.** Almost no filler. Clauses land cleanly. There is no "bloody." There is no "I was like." The meeting tics vanish when you sit down and type. "_A PDF file is basically a set of object serialised in a specific format similar to PostScript._" That's a Jason _written_ sentence: short, declarative, slightly casual ("basically"), no ornament.
- **"We" is the default narrator, not "I".** This surprised me, because I'd predicted the opposite. In "Building a PDF writer" you use "we" as the reader-and-writer-walking-together pronoun — _"Our initial goal is to be able to place text…,"_ _"we'll just start with the overall layout…,"_ _"we'll add the catalog…"_. "I" appears only when you're telling a story _about yourself_ ("While working on an app that deals with schedules I hit an issue…"). So the live posts using "we" aren't wrong per se — but they use "we" as a _company_ pronoun (corporate plural), whereas your blog's "we" is a _tutorial_ pronoun (you and me). Those read very differently.
- **You're still casual, just more selectively.** _"funky formatting,"_ _"decent amount of complexity,"_ _"a clever little nugget,"_ _"all those puzzle-solving pleasure chemicals in my brain had subsided,"_ _"Of course now I'm creating a new schedule object on each render,"_ _"Urgh! all those props mixed in together. What if I have a lot more object props?"_ — this is your written humour. Drier than speech. More observational. Less swearing, more self-aware bemusement.
- **You tell stories in past tense, with specific detail.** The React.memo post opens: _"While working on an app that deals with schedules I hit an issue where my controlled input for the schedule name was slow as molasses…"_ That's classic Jason-written: immediate situation ("I hit an issue"), one vivid image ("slow as molasses"), then straight to the problem. The meeting equivalent would be much loopier.
- **You do something the live makesPDF posts don't: you admit missteps in-line.** The flip in the React.memo post is the whole move of the piece — you solve the problem, walk away, come back and realise your solution was overcomplicated, and rewrite it. The post's structure literally has a section called **"The flip."** That level of "here's the thing I got wrong and then un-did" is very much your voice and is almost entirely missing from the live blog posts.
- **You close with a word, not a paragraph.** The React.memo post ends on a single italicised word: _KISS_. The testing post ends on a tip and a smiley: _"Jest will make writing and running your tests simpler and more enjoyable. 8)"_. No sweeping generalisation. No "lessons that generalise" section. The post is finished when you've said the thing.
- **Lists are fine, but you don't turn prose into bullets.** The testing post uses bullets where it should (recommendations, gotchas) and prose where it should (explanations, reasoning). The live makesPDF posts over-use bullets and tables in places where a paragraph would be more honest.
- **Headings are functional, not rhetorical.** Your blog headings: _"Services"_, _"Functional Testing"_, _"Unit Tests"_, _"Javascript"_, _"Mocking"_, _"Nocking"_, _"The flip"_. The live makesPDF headings: _"What was timeless"_, _"Why this shape works"_, _"What I think I've finally learned"_, _"Lessons that probably generalise"_. Yours are concrete; the current ones are editorialising.
- **You're not afraid to say "I don't know" in writing.** The Go cache post and the testing post both have moments of "this is what I worked out, here's where I'm still unsure." The live posts never admit any uncertainty.

### Vocabulary tells (written)

- **"basically"** — you lean on this in tutorials. _"A PDF file is basically a set of object serialised…"_ It signals "I'm simplifying, don't yell at me." Warm and disarming.
- **"naturally"** (italicised for irony) — _"so *naturally* I figured I'd just use that to cache the altered schedule objects."_ That's your dry-written version of "of course I over-engineered this, what did you expect."
- **"nice"**, **"goodness"** — _"for async/await goodness,"_ _"everything was good and I grabbed a coffee."_ Cheap positive words deployed knowingly.
- **"of course"** — marks the moment you (or the reader) should have seen the next problem coming.
- **Colloquial contractions kept in** — "don't," "we'll," "we've," "it's," "you'll." The live posts do this too; keep it.
- **Mild emoji / ASCII punctuation** — `8)` at the end of the testing post. You use visual punctuation sparingly but warmly.
- **"gotchya"** (in the title _"Code Splitting and Tree Shaking Webpack Gotchya"_) — you casualise even technical vocab. Signature Jason-on-the-page.
- **No "bloody."** The spoken signature doesn't survive the transition to the keyboard. Noted.
- **No "I was like" / no narrated dialogue** — the speech-narration device disappears in writing. You just describe what happened.
- **Almost no profanity.** The testing post drops one "8)" smiley. That's about the limit.

### Written-voice rhetorical moves

1. ~~**The TL;DR at the top.**~~ _Original claim, since rescinded (see §9a):_ React.memo opens _"Takeaway: keep your memoed components focussed and their props shallow, and prefer simple solutions over complexity."_ The original 4-post sample over-weighted this. Across the wider 16-post corpus, only [optimise-with-react-memo] uses `Takeaway:` and two posts use `TL;DR` ([code-tree-webpack], [gcf-to-appengine]); the other 13 dive straight into prose. **Labelled summary openers are not a rule.** Don't propagate the `Takeaway:` label, and don't add `TL;DR` openers as a default — if a summary line genuinely helps, write it as the opening sentence and move on.
2. **The "skip to the flip" in-page link.** You use anchor links inside posts to let the reader jump to the interesting part. This is a blogger move, not a documentation writer's move. Worth preserving in any rewrite.
3. **Telling the false-start first.** You routinely show the approach that didn't quite work, _then_ the one that did. This is story structure, and it's missing from the current makesPDF posts (which tend to present only the final clean architecture).
4. **Short closing word or phrase.** _KISS._ _8)_. _That's probably enough for today._ _We'll need to add some more objects… but that's probably enough for today._ Soft landings. Not "Lessons That Probably Generalise."
5. **Embedded quotes / blockquotes for primary sources.** The how-to-create-PDFs post opens with a big quote from Adam Flaherty. You use blockquotes to credit sources rather than to editorialise yourself. Current makesPDF posts use blockquotes to editorialise ("_Any request that does not return a final, usable PDF must not deduct credits._") — that's a different move and reads more rhetorically.
6. **Casual technical shorthand.** _"NP:"_ (note to reader), _"ie,"_ abbreviations unpacked inline. You treat the reader as a peer who can catch up, not a novice who needs everything defined.

### What this means concretely

The **live makesPDF posts sound more formal than your blog ever did**, which is ironic because they're on a site you own talking about a project you made. The current posts have absorbed a certain "AI-assisted technical marketing" register — clean, slightly breathless, fond of tables and principle-bullets. Your actual written voice is:

- **More patient** (willing to walk through a thing step by step)
- **More sheepish** (willing to say "I over-engineered this and had to un-do it")
- **Less declarative** ("basically," "of course," "naturally" as hedges)
- **Less structured** (fewer tables, fewer bulleted-lesson lists, more prose)
- **Warmer in small ways** (emoji, italicised asides, tutorial "we" instead of company "we")

This is also a voice that's more honest about the actual creative process, which for a blog about _rebuilding a 14-year-old project four times_ is probably the emotionally correct register.

## 3. What this means for the blog posts

The four posts are currently written in a voice that is **technically accurate, architecturally crisp, and emotionally absent**. They read like a polished technical-marketing writer ghost-wrote them. The ideas are yours. The _tissue_ between the ideas is not.

### The biggest single gap

Your actual written voice (the lecstor.com one) has a move the makesPDF posts never make: **showing the false start**. _"I thought this was the right answer, built it, looked at it, realised I'd over-engineered it, threw half of it out."_ That IS your project. The whole makesPDF story is a sequence of those. But the live posts present the final architecture as if it arrived on a silver platter. Adding even one "here's what I tried first" moment to each post would change the voice more than any other single edit.

### The second biggest gap

The current posts **editorialise in blockquotes** and **conclude with numbered lessons**. Your actual blog never does either. You use blockquotes for other people's words, and you conclude with a short word or a tip, not a five-point principle summary. The "Lessons that probably generalise" section in `ai-dsl-journey.md` is specifically the most un-Jason section across all four posts.

### ~~The third biggest gap~~ (rescinded)

The original analysis identified "no TL;DR opener" as a gap and recommended adding `Takeaway:` lines to most posts. After analysing the wider corpus (§9), this recommendation is rescinded — `Takeaway:` only appears on one of the 16 canonical posts ([optimise-with-react-memo]), and `TL;DR` only on two ([code-tree-webpack], [gcf-to-appengine]). Most posts open with prose. Don't add labelled summary openers as a default; if a post benefits from a one-line setup, write it as a normal sentence and move on.

### A note on "we" vs "I"

My first read of the transcripts said "swap 'we' to 'I' everywhere." After reading the blog, that's too strong. Your written _"we"_ is the tutorial-we (you and the reader walking through a thing), and it's natural to your voice. The fix isn't "replace all we with I" — it's **distinguishing tutorial-we from company-we**. Where the current posts say _"We ship three pieces that together…"_ that's company-we and it's false (solo project). Where the current posts say _"We had three sources of truth"_ that's closer to tutorial-we and reads fine. Use "I" for the stuff that's actually you alone; keep "we" for the stuff where you're walking the reader through a problem.

## 5. Small, universal edits to make across all four posts

Things that would consistently sound more like your written voice, applied everywhere:

1. ~~**Add a `Takeaway:` one-liner at the top of each post.**~~ **Rescinded** — see §9a. The original rule was extrapolated from a single post. Across the 16-post corpus, only one uses `Takeaway:` and two use `TL;DR`; most just dive in. Don't add labelled summary openers as a rule.
2. **Distinguish tutorial-"we" from company-"we".** Tutorial-we ("we'll start with the catalog, then walk through the validator") — keep. Company-we ("we shipped three pieces") — flip to "I" or rephrase. For a solo project, company-we reads slightly dishonest.
3. **Show at least one false start per post.** Your whole blog is built around "here's what I tried, it didn't quite work, here's what I did instead." The live posts present only the final clean architecture. Adding one failed-attempt moment per post (DSL compression realisation, the JSON-or-DSL dual interface, the three sources of truth drifting) would change the register more than any other edit.
4. **Replace `Lessons that generalise` sections with short closing beats.** Your blog closes on a word (_KISS_, _8)_, _"probably enough for today"_), not a numbered principle list. One short paragraph, then stop.
5. **Use "basically" / "naturally" / "of course" as hedges.** These are your written-voice tells. _"A PDF file is basically a set of serialised objects."_ _"Naturally I over-engineered it."_ _"Of course now I'm creating a new object on every render."_ A sprinkling of these lightens the prose without losing precision.
6. **Replace editorial blockquotes with narrative.** Blockquoted principles ("Any request that does not return a final, usable PDF must not deduct credits") read as pronouncements. Your blog uses blockquotes for _other people's_ words. Rewrite the principle-quotes into normal prose and save blockquotes for the podcast attribution, the CPAN POD quote in the history post, etc.
7. **Concrete section headings, not editorial ones.** _"What was timeless"_ → _"What survived"_. _"Why this shape works"_ → _"What we get out of it"_. _"Lessons that probably generalise"_ → cut the section; replace with one closing paragraph.
8. **Cut adjective-adjective noun phrases.** _"Compact builder DSL,"_ _"self-contained markdown file,"_ _"shareable skill file,"_ _"production-quality templates."_ Your blog says "the DSL," "the skill file." The extra adjective is marketing tissue.
9. **Allow one moment of admitted uncertainty per post.** "I'm still not sure this is the right shape." "This might not survive the next rewrite." "The jury's still out on whether X was the right call." Your real blog admits this; the live posts never do.
10. **Cut or reduce tables.** Your blog uses tables almost never; when it does it's for truly tabular data. The current live posts use tables as claim-containers ("Era / Pain / Corrective in next era"). A few of those would be better as prose. Keep the rate-limits / endpoint-comparison tables — those _are_ tabular data. Cut the "lessons" tables.

## 8. Corrections from the first calibration round

After the first pair of edits (`ai-pdfs-in-one-page.md` + `fourteen-years-of-pdfs.md`), Jason flagged several things to carry forward into the remaining posts:

- **Don't invent mechanism.** Phrases like "I opened my editor and counted the tokens" or "I leave it running for an hour" add colour but fabricate specifics that aren't true. When the substance of a beat is real but the mechanism isn't known, describe the beat without inventing how it happened.
- **Don't invent _motivations_ either.** Related but distinct: don't take an observation from elsewhere in the post (e.g. "the upstream PDF library was a dead end" from a What-was-incidental bullet) and promote it into a _reason I stopped doing the thing_. The real reason things get shut down is usually much more boring than the writerly version — for PDFUnicorn it was "no customers, hosting kept costing money." Default to the boring honest answer. Ecosystem-philosophising is almost always my invention, not Jason's lived reason.
- **"Throughline" is not a Jason word.** Cut. Same goes for any writerly abstractions that don't appear in his actual blog — if it sounds like a craft-writing term, it's probably mine, not his.
- **Clean up quoted speech; don't transcribe it.** When quoting from meetings (the podcast-provenance beat, the "this is gold" beat, etc.) preserve the substance but strip the ums, false-starts, and repeated words. Jason's written voice doesn't transcribe his spoken voice verbatim. The quote becomes a paraphrase or a cleaned-up line, not a dictaphone dump.
- **`docs/blog/` is not drafts-by-Jason.** Those are the original AI-written posts. `apps/web/content/blog/` is _another AI's rewrite_ of the same source material, also not by Jason. No version of these posts currently has Jason's voice in it. That means: nothing in the live posts is load-bearing as "Jason's original intent" — if a factual claim sounds off, it's probably AI invention (e.g. the "staffing Perl jobs" line in Era 1 — he was always solo).
- **"Us" is fine when it means the service.** The earlier pass over-flipped "we" → "I". When the sentence is describing a side of the wire (the service, the API surface, the catalog, the Worker config), "us" / "we" is correct — a service is a "we" even when operated by one person. Only flip to "I" when the sentence is about a decision or action Jason took alone (e.g. "I briefly offered a dual JSON-or-DSL interface; I removed it").
- **Don't over-narrow the takeaway.** A closer like _"Small DSL. Single markdown file. That's the whole trick."_ reduces a whole-shape post to one component. When the post is about the whole architecture (DSL + catalog + skill + deterministic API), the closer should reflect the whole shape.
- **Redundant openers.** If the Takeaway says "four attempts, fourteen years," the prose opener saying the same thing is noise. Pick one.

These are not voice rules so much as calibration fences — things that, unchecked, make AI-rewritten-prose sound like AI-rewritten-prose-trying-to-sound-human, which is worse than the original register.

The 2026 posts (`four-goes-at-the-same-idea`, `the-builder-i-didnt-build`, `catalog-dsl-skill`, `sub-second-no-eval-no-browser`, `makespdf-vscode-extension`) are AI-written and are referenced here only as the comparison surface — they are **not** voice sources.

[gcf-to-appengine] is all `I` because it narrates his own journey; [building-a-pdf-writer] is all tutorial `we` because it teaches a thing; [reusable-react-components] mixes both because it does both (lessons from his own work + how-to tips for the reader).

### 9b. New stylometric markers — the loudest AI tells when absent

#### The two-dot trail-off (`..`)

This is the single most distinctive marker the original analysis missed. Two dots, not three; the trail-off equivalent of speech's "yeah look" or "but yeah":

> _When searching for Perl references on the 'net, please make sure you restrict your search to the last 12 months.. there has been extensive progress in recent times._

> _Tightly constrained by the box, I wondered how I could install a 2.6 mongo shell on my Debian system without upgrading the whole shebang.. turns out there's no need.. just download the tar version of MongoDB and run the shell straight from there.._

> _At this point I reassessed things.._

> _On the plus side it'll all be there if I ever decide server-side rendering is the way to go.._

> _Learning Go, so started recording references to all the useful things I find so I can find them again when I need them.._

Present 2011–2022 in essentially every prose-bearing post. Completely absent from the 2026 AI rewrites, which substitute em-dashes or full ellipses or just chop the sentence. **If a Jason post contains zero `..`, voice has drifted.** Rough budget: ≥1 per substantial paragraph that's doing pivot/trail-off work.

#### ASCII smileys (`8)`, `8(`)

> _Jest will make writing and running your tests simpler and more enjoyable. 8)_ — [testing-js]
>
> _well, look at that sky.. 8) for my latest project, PDFUnicorn…_ — [using-catalyst-with-a-fat-model]
>
> _having my app to hit the cloud functions directly 8(_ — [gcf-to-appengine]

ASCII smileys, not Unicode emoji. Old-internet register. Appears 2011 through 2019 continuously. Don't substitute `🙂` or `🙁`.

#### `ok,` / `Ok,` as paragraph or sentence opener

Lowercase or casual upper `ok,` to pivot — the written cousin of "yeah no wait, actually...":

> _Ok, so why not regular methods in the Controllers which are called from your Actions?_
>
> _ok, but I thought we were talking about Models?_
>
> _ok, finally, my current solution? the fat Model._

Survives the spoken→written transition more than the original analysis suggested.

#### `eg` / `ie` without dots

`eg I needed to add a tooltip…`, `eg \`map\`, \`filter\``. Never `e.g.` or `i.e.` or `for example`. Treat the reader as a peer.

#### Long sentences glued with `and`/`so`/`but`, no comma-chopping

> _I had had thoughts of using websockets for live updates on database changes and realised that that was never going to work with GCF._

> _I could add a service behind the load balancer and use it for websockets and at the same time solve the functions issue but then there didn't seem to be enough benefit from maintaining the cloud functions as well as a service so I opted to move everything to a service._

He doesn't chop these into clean clauses. AI rewrites do. Comma-spliced clauses are normal; clean topic-sentence-then-supporting-sentences paragraphs are not.

#### Parens for asides, not em-dashes

> _(`pick` vs a custom func is definitely purely down to preference)_
>
> _(unconfirmed if this was only with plain media queries)_
>
> _(bugga, it only dumps the last mesage..)_
>
> _(or download from [Golang Downloads](https://golang.org/dl/))_

Parens carry the aside, the qualifier, the wry comment. AI prefers em-dashes for the same job. **Em-dash count vs paren-aside count is a good drift gauge** — in the 2026 posts em-dashes do the asides and parens are scarce.

### 9c. Additional vocabulary tells

- **`goodness`** as a wry positive: `async/await goodness`, `non-blocking goodness`, `the goodness of modern programming practices`. Cheap positive, deployed knowingly.
- **`bugga`** as parenthetical Aussie mild expletive: `(bugga, it only dumps the last mesage..)`. Family of `bloody` from §1; survives into writing only as a parens-aside.
- **`for learnings`** / `for [noun]` framing: `For learnings I decided to use Google Cloud Functions`. Distinctively Australian.
- **`the latter` / `the former`**: casual, not stuffy. `I opted for the latter for the reduced cost and complexity.`
- **`a couple of`** for vague numbers; never `several` or `a number of`.
- **Time markers** (`today`, `currently`, `at the moment`, `recently`): posts are present-tense missives, not timeless reference docs. _"today we'll just start with the overall layout"_, _"Today I'm going to talk about using a fat model"_.
- **`magic spell`** for a trick: `In the end though, the magic spell turned out to be: …`. Affection for the cure.

### 9d. Genre distinctions

The corpus splits into roughly four shapes. AI rewrites tend to homogenise them all toward the opinion-piece register. Each one is allowed to be itself:

| Genre | Examples | Shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Short recipe / lookup** | [go-clear-cache], [zustand-vanilla-auto-methods], [react-disabled-button-onmouseleave], [mongodb-upgrade-24-to-26-in-debian] | ≤50 lines. One problem, one snippet, soft close. Often opens with the error or symptom. Closes with `Maybe it'll help you too.` energy. **Don't pad these into essays.** |
| **Cheatsheet / link collection** | [go-cheatsheet], [kafka-cheatsheet], [how-to-create-pdf-documents] | Code blocks and bullet links dominate. Prose minimal. Headings are commands or section names. Don't editorialise. |
| **Tutorial walkthrough** | [building-a-pdf-writer], [code-tree-webpack], [using-catalyst-with-a-fat-model], [reusable-react-components] | Step-by-step, tutorial-`we`, code alternating with explanation. Soft close (`that's probably enough for today`). |
| **Opinion / journey post** | [gcf-to-appengine], [optimise-with-react-memo], [testing-js], [something-about-perl] | Personal `I`, narrative arc, often a pivot/flip mid-post, short closing word/phrase (`*KISS*`, `8)`, `**do no harm**`). |

The 2026 makesPDF posts are all written as opinion/journey pieces. That genre is real Jason. But a small announcement (like the [makespdf-vscode-extension] post) belongs in the short-recipe shape — don't bloat it into a journey post.

### 9e. Punctuation discipline

- **Em-dashes are rare.** Periods + paragraph breaks for pivots; `..` for trail-offs; parens for asides. Em-dashes only for genuine apposition.
- **Two-dot trail-offs (`..`) appear in most prose-bearing posts.** If a new Jason-voice post has none, voice has drifted.
- **Curly quotes appear in older posts only** — likely an artefact of the editor he used at the time. Don't impose them on new content; don't strip them from old content.
- **ASCII smileys (`8)`, `8(`)** are allowed and load-bearing. Don't substitute Unicode emoji.
- **`*italic*` for asides** (`*naturally*`, `*KISS*`, `*TL;DR*`) used sparingly for irony or landing.
- **Bullet lists for items, numbered for sequences, prose for arguments.** No bullets-as-prose. Tables only for genuinely tabular data.

### 9f. What NOT to "fix"

A non-trivial part of voice is small imperfections an AI rewrite would automatically clean up:

- Typos in passing (`mesage`, double-`had`, lowercase-after-period) — leave them
- Inconsistent heading casing within a post (Title Case mixed with sentence case mixed with lowercase) — leave it
- `eg` / `ie` un-dotted — leave them
- Long comma-spliced clauses — leave them
- WIP markers in titles (`Reusable React Components [WIP]`) — allowed; he ships unfinished and says so
- Lowercase sentence-starts after a pivot (`ok, finally, my current solution? the fat Model.`) — intentional casualness

Treat these as features. Cleaning them turns the post stiffer than the original ever was.

The §3–8 critique holds up under the wider sample. If anything the gap is wider than the four-post analysis suggested:

- **Two-dot trail-offs in 2026 posts: zero.** Across all five.
- **Em-dash density** in the 2026 posts is roughly 3–4× higher than in the canonical corpus (rough sample count). Most em-dashes are doing the work parens do in real Jason.
- **Inline parentheticals** are scarce in 2026 posts; em-dashes have taken their job.
- **No 2026 post uses `eg`/`ie` un-dotted.** All use full `e.g.` / `i.e.` or `for example`.

### 9h. Practical fences for new posts

Distilled from the above. Not a checklist to mechanically apply, but a set of "if the post has none of these, it's not in voice yet" markers:

1. **At least one `..` (two-dot trail-off)** in any post that has more than two paragraphs of prose.
2. **Em-dash count ≤ paren-aside count.** If em-dashes are doing more work than parens, swap the parens back in.
3. **Don't open with a labelled summary (`Takeaway:`, `TL;DR`) as a rule.** Only 3 of 16 canonical posts do; the other 13 dive straight into prose. If a one-sentence summary genuinely helps, write it as an unlabelled opening sentence and move on.
4. **Cut meta-commentary that summarizes or signposts adjacent content.** No "That's the headline.", "This post is about X", "This post explains how Y", "The short version is...", "Stepping back, here's what X looks like", "To sum up..." — these are AI-rewrite tics. Real Jason lets the content stand; he doesn't introduce sections or wrap them up. The next H2 says what it has to say on its own.
5. **Headings are concrete and functional** (`What it does`, `Cache Clean`, `The flip`). Never editorial (`Why this shape works`, `The broader point`).
6. **One adjective per noun phrase, max.** "The DSL", not "a compact builder DSL".
7. **Cut "honestly" / "the honest version" / "to be honest".** The register is honest by default.
8. **Short recipe posts close softly** (`Maybe it'll help you too.`, `8)`, `*KISS*`) — don't append a "lessons that generalise" coda.
9. **Match the genre to the content** (§9d) — don't write a journey post when a recipe will do.
10. **Allow the small imperfections in §9f.** Don't clean them up out of habit.
11. **Use `eg` / `ie` un-dotted, `a couple of` for vague numbers, `the latter` / `the former` when toggling between options.** Cheap voice tells.
12. **Don't repeat substantive content across sections.** A bullet list followed by a table of the same items, or one section's conclusion restated at the start of the next — both are AI-rewrite failure modes. Pick one format per point. Repetition for genuine emphasis is fine; repetition because the structural element seemed nice is not.
13. **Don't frame posts around development status.** No phase numbers, "coming soon", "later phases", "in the middle of building it", or enumerating what isn't built yet. Posts are for general readers; phase tracking lives in the project's `plans/` docs. Write about what the project IS and what it does today. If a deliberate design choice happens to overlap with "what isn't there" (e.g. "no drag-and-drop editor — the AI is the front door"), frame it as the design choice.
